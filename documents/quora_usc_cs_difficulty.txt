Overview
The Computer Science (CS) major at USC (Viterbi School of Engineering and the USC Dornsife/CS joint program) is rigorous, well-resourced, and reputation-strong. It combines theoretical foundations, systems and software engineering, and a strong emphasis on research and industry connections. USC’s location in Los Angeles and its alumni network translate into robust internship and job pipelines, especially for gaming, entertainment tech, robotics, AI, and startups.

Program structure and tracks

Multiple degree homes: CS is offered through Viterbi (B.S.) and Dornsife (B.A.) with different core requirements and electives; Viterbi is more engineering-focused.
Core foundations: discrete math, data structures & algorithms, systems (computer organization/architecture, operating systems), programming languages/compilers, theory (automata/complexity), and software engineering.
Electives and specializations: AI/ML, graphics/games, security, networking, databases, HCI, robotics, vision. Plenty of research labs and capstone/project courses.
Hands-on opportunities: industry-sponsored projects, USC Games, research labs, entrepreneurship support (incubators, ITP/CS collaborations).
How hard are the classes?

Curriculum difficulty: solid-to-challenging. Expect steep learning curves in core courses—Data Structures & Algorithms, Systems/OS, Computer Architecture, and Theory are widely considered the toughest.
Grading and workload: competitive environment with substantial programming assignments, projects, and exams. Group projects are common and assess real engineering skills. Some courses curve; others are more absolute.
Pace: quarter system (if applicable) accelerates timeline for material mastery; semesters (for some programs) offer more breathing room. Upper-division electives can be very specialized and demanding.
Variation by instructor: difficulty and emphasis vary significantly by professor and TA team. Some instructors emphasize theory and proofs; others emphasize large-scale projects and engineering trade-offs.
What makes it hard (typical pain points)

Complexity of concepts: algorithms proofs/analysis, concurrency and synchronization in OS, low-level debugging in architecture, formal reasoning in theory.
Project scale: multi-week group projects with real systems integration, performance constraints, testing, and deployment.
Time management: multiple heavy courses and overlapping deadlines; extracurriculars (research, startups, clubs) add load.
Competitive peers: motivated cohort raises expectation and pace; collaboration helps but grade pressure exists.
What eases the difficulty

Strong support infrastructure: office hours, robust TA networks, discussion sections, tutoring centers, coding labs, and active student organizations (ACM, hackathon teams, game dev clubs).
Peer study culture: extensive collaboration networks, study groups, and open-source project participation.
Abundant resources: past course notes, recorded lectures, GitHub repos from prior semesters, and online material aligned to course topics.
Internship and research pathways: practical experience often makes coursework easier to grasp and more meaningful.
Outcomes and ROI

Placements: high internship and job placement rates at major tech firms, entertainment/VR studios, startups, and research labs. Average starting salaries are competitive nationally.
Research and graduate school: strong preparation for MS/PhD; active faculty-led labs with undergraduate research opportunities.
Transferable skills: algorithmic thinking, systems design, software engineering practices, and team-based development valued across industries.
Advice to succeed

Master fundamentals early: focus on data structures, algorithms, and discrete math first.
Prioritize projects: treat code quality, testing, documentation, and design as part of the grade.
Use campus resources: attend office hours, join study groups, and leverage previous semesters’ materials.
Balance load: limit simultaneous heavy technical courses when possible; use lighter electives or pass/fail options strategically.
Do internships/research early: practical experience accelerates learning and reduces abstract difficulty.
Typical student experiences (examples)

Example A: A student with prior programming experience found intro courses straightforward, but struggled with OS and architecture—used office hours and a student study group to pass with strong grades and secure a summer internship.
Example B: A student without CS background found the first year intense but accelerated via tutoring and bridge programs; by junior year they led a capstone and obtained competitive job offers.
Example C: A highly involved student balanced demanding classes with research by strategically taking one heavy systems/theory course per term and relied on team collaboration for project-heavy classes.
Bottom line
USC CS is academically demanding but well-supported, with high industry visibility and strong career outcomes. Difficulty stems from rigorous core topics, large projects, and a competitive peer group; however, abundant resources, engaged faculty, and practical opportunities make success achievable with disciplined study, collaboration, and early hands-on experience.

In my freshman year of school, I was in a dorm with some CS and engineering majors, and the engineers had a hierarchy for the difficulty of CS relative to other majors. They ranked engineering (electrical, mechanical) as the hardest, with CS below that (I don’t quite remember where they put civil engineering, but for some reason they seemed to consider that “different”), and I think below that was a math major. I don’t know where the physical sciences fit into this. Looking back on it, it’s striking to me that this wasn’t included, because I’d assume majoring in physics, chemistry, for example, would rank somewhere in this hierarchy.

The reason they said this was they’d seen how when engineering students couldn’t cut it, they’d often change majors to CS, because it was easier.

I’d say what they were talking about was somewhat accurate. I personally would’ve put a math major as more difficult than CS, and the physical sciences as more difficult than that, since as a CS major, I found the math classes challenging enough that I kind of sweated around them, and I found my physical science classes much more challenging. Whereas, I found most of my CS classes pretty easy. It got more challenging once I got into my upper division CS courses, but it also got a lot more interesting.

There was a lot more math in the upper-division courses, which validated the notion that “CS looks a lot like math.” However, the math it focused on had a lot to do with describing logic, and the characteristics of discrete domains. This provided a basis for discussing the effectiveness and correctness of algorithms, which was where a lot of CS was centered, when I was in school.

From my perspective and for undergraduate education, compared with CS curriculum provided by more prestigious universities, the CS classes at USC are significantly easier.

One example is CSCI360, Intro to Artificial Intelligence. This is a required class for generic CS major students at USC, and it only introduces really basic concepts to students. For the whole semester, we just wrote three AI-related programs, and two of them are pretty easy (take no more than 2 hours to finish if implemented correctly). The lecture contents are not challenging enough either. I found the course CS231n provided by Stanford is much harder in comparison, and actually has better illustration of many more useful concepts.

Also, it’s really easy to get good grades for CS major students at USC. There’s severe grade inflation. I remember that approximately 60% of students got A in CSCI201, Principles of Software Development. It’s because that the professor gave so many extra credits and I got like near 100% at the end of the semester.

But anyway, there are still some really good classes like ITP435 (Professional C++) and CSCI420(Computer Graphics) though they are technical electives. Many CS Games classes are fun too. I really enjoyed them. The CS curriculum here at USC does help students build up skills necessary in the industry, and it’s easier for students to get internships given the good location. Overall, it’s a decent program but definitely not the best.

Above are just my personal opinion. Hope that helps :)
